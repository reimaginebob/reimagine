# 11e. How an Offer Is Put Together

An offer is more than a base salary, and the parts that aren't base are where the real value, and the real traps, hide. This chapter is the anatomy: every element that can show up in a white-collar offer, what each one is worth, and the question to ask about each. Reimagine's Offer & Negotiation section uses this same logic to price the stable parts, and My Coach draws on it to reason about your specific situation. Figures dated 2026 are IRS or legal limits current for that year; they move, so confirm the current number when it matters.

## The cash

**Base salary** is the only fully guaranteed, non-probabilistic number in the whole offer, so it anchors everything else. Geographic pay zones are real: some employers (GitLab publishes its formula; Google and Meta use metro zones) can pay six figures differently for the identical level by location. Ask which pay zone applies to your address, whether it is locked at hire, and what happens if you relocate later — many employers adjust base down on a move to a lower-cost area. If a range was posted (about ten states and several cities now require it), ask where your offer lands in it and why.

**Sign-on bonuses** are usually one-time and almost always carry a clawback: repay some or all if you leave within a window, commonly 12 months. Taxed as supplemental wages at a flat 22% withholding (which is a withholding rate, not your final tax). Ask the exact clawback window and the proration math, whether a layoff excuses repayment, and whether what you'd owe back is gross or net of the tax already withheld — that last one is a frequent and expensive surprise.

**Annual bonuses** come in three flavors: guaranteed (contractually owed, common as a first-year make-whole), target (a percentage of base at full attainment, not guaranteed), and discretionary (no formula). The near-universal trap is an "active employment on the payout date" clause: a fully earned bonus can be forfeited by leaving before the check is cut, usually in Q1 of the next year. Ask the target percentage, whether year one is guaranteed, the historical company-multiplier range, and the exact payout date and its forfeiture condition.

**Commission plans** quote OTE (on-target earnings) = base + variable at full attainment, which is a target, not a promise. Watch the base/variable mix, the quota-setting method, and draws: a recoverable draw is an advance you repay if commissions don't cover it; a non-recoverable draw is a floor. Ask for quota and territory in writing before signing, the draw type and what happens to an unrecovered balance at termination, and the churn-clawback window (commission reversed if a deal is refunded or churns).

The question that runs across all of cash comp: what exact event triggers forfeiture, and what is the exact date and condition for payment. The gap between "earned" and "paid" is where most disputes start.

## The equity

**RSUs** vest over four years with a one-year cliff, with refresh grants layered in from year two or three. They are taxed as ordinary income at each vesting date on the value then, not at grant. Public companies use single-trigger vesting (time alone settles the grant); private companies typically use double-trigger (time plus a liquidity event like an IPO or acquisition), which protects you from owing tax on stock you can't sell but means leaving before that event can forfeit otherwise time-vested shares. Unvested RSUs are almost always forfeited on departure, with no leverage to negotiate afterward, so it has to be addressed before signing.

**Stock options** split into ISOs (employees only, better tax treatment) and NSOs. The load-bearing detail is the post-termination exercise window: historically just 90 days to decide whether to pay to exercise (plus possible AMT) or forfeit vested equity. A 90-day window with no early-exercise option, at a private company with no clear exit timeline, is the commonest way vested equity gets lost. Ask whether the grant is an ISO or NSO, the exact exercise window, and the most recent 409A price.

**ESPP** (employee stock purchase plan) lets you buy stock through payroll deduction at around a 15% discount, often with a lookback to the lower price. Given the built-in discount, it is close to free money and worth enrolling in even minimally.

**Private-company equity** is where face-value math misleads. The 409A valuation that sets the tax price is deliberately conservative and goes stale between financings. The bigger issue is the liquidation-preference stack: preferred investors are paid back before common stockholders, which is where employees sit, so a headline exit valuation can still leave common stock worth little. Discount illiquid private equity heavily against a public company's liquid shares. Ask what has been raised and on what preference terms, and treat an early-stage grant as a high-variance, low-expected-value instrument unless your savings cushion can support that exposure. A refusal to disclose the preference stack or total dilution is a warning sign.

**On an acquisition**, equity can be assumed, cashed out, or accelerated. Single-trigger acceleration vests on the deal closing; double-trigger (more protective) vests only if the deal closes and you're let go without cause within a window after. Ask which applies.

## The benefits

**Health insurance** means little as "we offer medical." It needs four numbers: the monthly premium and the employer/employee split, the deductible, the out-of-pocket maximum (2026 ACA cap: $10,600 self-only / $21,200 family), and the copay/coinsurance structure. Request the Summary of Benefits and Coverage — a standardized document — from each employer to compare like for like.

**HSA and FSA.** An HSA requires a high-deductible plan, is triple tax-advantaged, portable, and rolls over indefinitely; an FSA works with any plan but is largely use-it-or-lose-it. Many employers seed the HSA directly ($500–$1,000 self-only, $1,000–$2,000 family is common), which is real money on top of your own contributions and worth asking about.

**401(k).** Get the exact match formula — 100% up to 3% of pay, or 50% up to 6%, both land the employer at 3% — and the vesting schedule on the match (a cliff or a graded schedule; employee contributions are always yours immediately). A mega backdoor Roth is high-value where it exists, mostly at large tech and finance employers, and worth asking about since most plans don't support it.

**Life and disability.** Basic employer life insurance is typically 1x salary. Long-term disability replaces roughly half to two-thirds of salary after an elimination period. Disability is far more likely across a career than death, and easy to overlook while focused on health and life coverage.

**PTO.** "Unlimited" deserves a skeptical eye: people on unlimited policies take about 16 days a year versus 14 on accrued plans, only a two-day difference, and unlimited PTO forfeits the cash-out value that accrued PTO has at termination in many states. Ask what departing employees take and whether unused accrued time pays out when you leave.

**Other things worth pricing.** Tuition reimbursement (often capped near $5,250/year, the tax-free threshold), student-loan assistance, and — for anyone who needs it — visa or immigration sponsorship, whose real employer cost of $10,000–$25,000+ can outweigh most negotiable salary differences.

## The fine print

**At-will employment** is standard boilerplate in nearly every US offer; it is not itself a reason for alarm and not where to spend negotiating capital.

**Non-competes** are governed entirely by state law — there is no federal ban as of 2026. Several states (including California, Minnesota, Oklahoma, North Dakota, and DC) void them outright; others heavily restrict them, often below a salary threshold. If you're presented with one, scrutinize its duration, its geographic scope ("worldwide" is a red flag for a remote role), and how narrowly "competitor" is defined. Many companies will narrow the scope on request for an individual contributor.

**Non-solicit and non-disparagement** clauses survive even in states that ban non-competes; a non-disparagement clause in a severance agreement should carve out statements to government agencies.

**IP and invention-assignment agreements** assign work created "during employment" to the employer, language broad enough to sweep in side projects. Fill in the Prior Inventions Schedule to exclude anything you built beforehand; leaving it blank can be read as having nothing to exclude. Several states require a carve-out for inventions made entirely on your own time — confirm the notice is present.

**Contingencies** — background check, references, I-9, sometimes credential checks — usually resolve after you accept, occasionally after you've already resigned a prior job. If one fails, the offer can be pulled with no recourse. The practical rule: don't resign a current job or make an irreversible commitment until every contingency has formally cleared.

## What it's worth

A comparable total value adds up like this: **base + guaranteed cash + risk-adjusted bonus + equity expected value + monetized benefits.**

- **Bonus and commission** should never be counted at the headline target. Multiply the target by a realistic attainment rate — team-wide historical attainment, which a hiring manager can share, is a far better number than the OTE headline — and prorate a partial first year.
- **Equity.** Public RSUs are shares times current price, by vesting tranche. Private equity should be discounted by stage: an early-stage grant carries a much steeper discount than a late-stage, pre-IPO one, because the preference stack is larger relative to the company and liquidity is further off. Treat an early grant as high-variance.
- **Benefits** turn into dollars against your own paycheck. What you pay for health is a real cost that leaves your check every month, so it's subtracted; the money that comes back to you is added — the 401(k) match (if you contribute enough to capture it), an employer HSA contribution, and PTO valued at your daily rate (salary over about 260 working days) times the days. The employer's own share of the premium doesn't enter the math; it never touches your wallet. This is why two offers a few thousand dollars apart in salary can flip once the health premium is counted: a higher salary with a costlier plan can net less than a lower salary with fully paid coverage.
- **Comparing offers** with very different shapes — a high-base, low-equity role against a low-base, high-equity one at a risky startup — works best when you risk-adjust each one, normalize only the cash components for cost of living (equity isn't location-dependent), and look at a conservative case and an optimistic case side by side. An honest range beats one falsely precise number.
